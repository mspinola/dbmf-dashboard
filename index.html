Who else is in these markets

Two questions, two marks. The bar answers who owns this market: net position as a share of open interest. The percentile answers how stretched that group is against its own past year. They differ. A bar can be long but mid-percentile (large but normal for that market) or short but 98th percentile (small in absolute terms, extreme for them). You need both, which is why each bar sits inside its band.

Full bars are whole sides. The solid tip is the opinion-holders; the textured segment carries the rest of that side of the market (intermediaries, unclassified reportables). Because everyone's positions net to zero, the two full tips on a row should nearly mirror each other, which is a quick check that the row is drawn right. The gap between them is non-reportables, the small traders below the CFTC's reporting threshold. They are kept off the bars on purpose: they are not the systematic crowd the speculative bar tracks, and where they are large (gold, long) they are a separate sign of crowding, shown in the tooltip and the detail table.

Read the pair, not the single bar. A market is crowded when the two sides confirm each other: speculators near the top of their range while the hedger side sits near the bottom of theirs. When both sides read near their medians, positioning has nothing to say, and the percentile column is telling you that too.

Is DBMF crowded, and with whom? Find the fund's bar in the left column, then look at the speculative bar on the same row. Same direction with a high speculative percentile and a low hedger percentile means the fund is in a late-stage consensus trade with unwind risk. Same direction with speculators mid-range means the trade has room and company is thin. What this pair of bars cannot tell you is whether the two sides arrived at the same place together: a row can show both short today after a year of moving in opposite directions. The panel below measures that over 54 weeks, and it is the better answer to "with whom", because it names the trader group the fund actually moves with rather than assuming it is the speculative one. It usually is not.

Discount what the panel flags. Treasury rows are basis flow. Any row where the hatched segment does most of the work means the opposing position is client-flow hedging, not an opinion. A 2nd-percentile reading on a book that never had a view is structure, not conviction.

An extreme is a condition, not a trigger. Extreme positioning arrives in episodes and can sit pegged for months. A 98th percentile means the trade is crowded and the fuel for a violent unwind exists; it says nothing about timing. It is where a setup forms, and the market still has to crack before it means anything.

Holdings

How DBMF works #

It copies what the big trend-following funds are doing, from their returns, in a handful of futures
1 · TargetThe SG CTA Index: about 20 of the largest trend-following funds (CTAs), with their estimated fees added back.
→
2 · Infer positionsFind the long and short weights in a few liquid futures whose daily returns best match the index's over the last several weeks (published accounts say four weeks to 60 days).
→
3 · Hold themBuy and sell those futures, keep the cash in T-bills as collateral, and re-estimate every week.

Reading positions from returns

The managers' positions are not public, but their returns are, and returns reveal net exposure: a fund that gains on days the 10-year note falls is short it. This is returns-based style analysis. DBi solves it as a constrained optimization, because ten partly overlapping markets against a few weeks of daily returns is a thin regression on its own.

Why ten futures can be enough

The managers' quirks cancel. Twenty CTAs each trade a hundred or more markets with their own signals; averaged together their idiosyncratic bets largely offset, and what is left is mostly exposure to a few large trends in rates, the dollar, equities, energy and gold.

A few liquid contracts cover most of it. DBi says these markets account for about 90% or more of the managers' pre-fee returns over time. That is the issuer's figure. What this tracker can check is narrower: the fund's own daily return tracks the sum of its futures positions at a correlation of about 0.99, so the disclosed futures explain DBMF itself (see "What each market earned").

Fees are the edge. Matching the managers' gross exposure for 0.85% a year instead of the industry's typical 2% and 20% keeps the difference, which DBi puts at hundreds of basis points over time.

Ten in practice, not by rule. Nothing public limits the fund to ten markets: it is actively managed and may use liquid futures and forwards across equities, rates, currencies and commodities. In practice it has held the same ten throughout this tracker's history (the line under the three steps checks this against the data every day). The index version, SDMF, is defined as ten contracts.

Where it falls short

It is late at turns. The weights come from the last few weeks of returns, so they show where the managers were. In a sharp reversal DBMF still holds the old trend after the managers have cut it. Gold in 2026 is the example on this page: long into the February spike, then short through the slide.

The estimate is noisy. The three Treasury contracts move almost together and there are only a few weeks of data behind each estimate, so weights can jump, and some jumps are re-estimates rather than the managers changing their minds.

Markets it does not hold. Whatever the managers make in grains, softs, smaller currencies or non-US rates is missed. DBi has said missing softs and other markets cost about 2 points in 2024, and missing the Mexican peso and Canadian short rates hurt in early 2023. DBi's position is to live with these misses rather than chase them: a small set of deep, liquid contracts keeps trading costs low and the estimate stable. It describes those episodes as a cost of the design, not as a reason to add markets.

It copies the twenty biggest funds, not trend following as a whole. When they crowd into the same trade, DBMF crowds in with them.
