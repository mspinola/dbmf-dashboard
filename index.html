What drove the book

Shares, not returns. Each bar is one asset class's share of everything that moved in the window: its contribution divided by the sum of all four contributions in absolute value. A class that lost money reads as a negative share of what moved rather than disappearing into a net figure. The underlying contributions are in "Show the numbers", deliberately not labelled as performance.

What is being measured is a replica, not the fund. Every snapshot carries each position's contract count and its price, so a held position's P&L over an interval is computable. Summing that by class gives the return of holding the disclosed book between disclosures. The fund trades in between, weekly by design, so this is a different strategy from the one you own.

How closely it reconciles, measured. Two corrections were needed before this question could be answered at all. The fund page quotes the previous session's settlement, so an interval measured here has to be compared against the fund's move one session earlier; correcting that takes the mean error per interval from 0.878 to 0.045 percentage points, and lagging by two sessions is as bad as lagging by none, so the alignment is identified rather than chosen. And a sum of interval returns is not a compounded return: over a +25% year those differ by about two points. With both fixed, the replica reconciles to well under a point a year with no carry or fee adjustment applied at all. The live figure is in the note above the chart.

Why that residual is not the cost of the fund's trading. The per-interval error has a standard deviation of 0.13 points, so a year of intervals accumulates about 2 points of pure noise, and the observed residual is well inside that. Separately, T-bill income is invisible to this method and worth roughly +2.3% a year against about −0.6% of fees, so the replica ought to sit about 1.7 points below the fund before trading enters the picture, and it sits less than a point below. Two effects of opposite sign, each larger than the residual, fit the data equally well. An earlier version of this panel put the gap at three points and blamed the fund's trading; both the size and the cause were errors in the measurement here, not findings about the fund.

A caution about the fund's published figures. The "+13.06% YTD" shown on the fund page is as of the most recent MONTH END, 31 August, not the date beside it. Its quarter-end companion, +10.11%, is 30 June. Comparing a figure measured to today against either of those understates by whatever the fund has done since, which for September 2026 alone was about four points.

What it cannot see. T-bill rows carry no price, so the cash leg contributes nothing here even though it is roughly a fifth of the fund's actual return. A position that rolled to a new contract has no comparable price pair, so that root's interval is dropped, about 37 root-days a year. And MFS and MES have no futures series to check against at all, so the equity share is the least trustworthy of the four.

Who else is in these markets

Two questions, two marks. The bar answers who owns this market: net position as a share of open interest. The percentile answers how stretched that group is against its own past year. They differ. A bar can be long but mid-percentile (large but normal for that market) or short but 98th percentile (small in absolute terms, extreme for them). You need both, which is why each bar sits inside its band.

Full bars are whole sides. The solid tip is the opinion-holders; the textured segment carries the rest of that side of the market (intermediaries, unclassified reportables). Because everyone's positions net to zero, the two full tips on a row should nearly mirror each other, which is a quick check that the row is drawn right. The gap between them is non-reportables, the small traders below the CFTC's reporting threshold. They are kept off the bars on purpose: they are not the systematic crowd the speculative bar tracks, and where they are large (gold, long) they are a separate sign of crowding, shown in the tooltip and the detail table.

Read the pair, not the single bar. A market is crowded when the two sides confirm each other: speculators near the top of their range while the hedger side sits near the bottom of theirs. When both sides read near their medians, positioning has nothing to say, and the percentile column is telling you that too.

Is DBMF crowded, and with whom? Find the fund's bar in the left column, then look at the speculative bar on the same row. Same direction with a high speculative percentile and a low hedger percentile means the fund is in a late-stage consensus trade with unwind risk. Same direction with speculators mid-range means the trade has room and company is thin. What this pair of bars cannot tell you is whether the two sides arrived at the same place together: a row can show both short today after a year of moving in opposite directions. The panel below measures that over 54 weeks, and it is the better answer to "with whom", because it names the trader group the fund actually moves with rather than assuming it is the speculative one. It usually is not.

Discount what the panel flags. Treasury rows are basis flow. Any row where the hatched segment does most of the work means the opposing position is client-flow hedging, not an opinion. A 2nd-percentile reading on a book that never had a view is structure, not conviction.

An extreme is a condition, not a trigger. Extreme positioning arrives in episodes and can sit pegged for months. A 98th percentile means the trade is crowded and the fuel for a violent unwind exists; it says nothing about timing. It is where a setup forms, and the market still has to crack before it means anything.
